A gateway is a small TURN server that runs on a desktop participant's own machine, made reachable from the internet through the participant's router. It is not a third-party relay: it is the participant's own front door, and only the people in that participant's session get a key.

## Why a front door helps

Take Ben, on a phone hotspot behind a carrier NAT that picks a new random port for every destination, and Dani, who plays from the desktop app at home. Ben's NAT makes his address unpredictable, and Dani's home router drops packets it was not expecting. A direct route between them usually fails.

Dani's router can do something about that: it supports UPnP. So Dani's app asks the router to forward a port to her machine, and runs a TURN server behind it. Now Ben has an address that accepts his packets. His browser connects to Dani's gateway, the gateway hands the packets to Dani, and the path is reported as `gateway`. In the lab this is scenario `two-peer-desktop-host`, three passes out of three, with 37 to 66 KB of gate traffic per run.

The same front door works for Omar, whose office blocks all UDP: his browser reaches Dani's gateway with TURN over TCP (scenario `udpblock-gateway`).

## How the gateway becomes reachable

When the desktop app first asks for its gateway, Freehop:

1. starts a TURN server on the machine's LAN address, listening on UDP and TCP port 3478 by default;
2. asks the router for port mappings, trying **PCP** (RFC 6887), then **NAT-PMP** (RFC 6886), then **UPnP IGD** v1 and v2;
3. requests the same external port as the internal one, for the listener and, on demand, for each relayed port (49160 to 49359 by default), and releases a relayed port's mapping as soon as its allocation ends.

A machine with a public address skips the mapping step. If the router reports a private or carrier-grade NAT address as its external address, the gateway is not offered at all, because nobody outside could reach it. If you forward ports by hand, pass `externalAddress` and `portMapping: false`.

:::note[Not every router can do this]

Routers without PCP, NAT-PMP or UPnP cannot map a port, and participants behind carrier-grade NAT have no public address to map. Their desktop app then joins as an ordinary peer, and the other steps of the [ladder](./paths.mdx) (host node, forwarding) remain available. Port mapping has been qualified against miniupnpd in the lab and against simulated routers in unit tests; physical routers are not yet verified.

:::

## Keys for one room and one person

A gateway never hands out a general password. Dani's client mints a credential for each peer, for this room only, following the TURN REST convention:

```text
username   = "<unix expiry>:<first 8 characters of the room tag>:<recipient peer id>"
credential = base64( HMAC-SHA1( gateway secret, username ) )
```

- The credential travels to Ben inside a sealed `caps` envelope, so the gate never sees it.
- Credentials go only to authenticated room members, never to a peer that a gate merely announced.
- They expire after **2 hours** and are renewed at half-life.
- The gateway accepts only rooms its owner told it to serve. On a kick, the removed peer's credentials are revoked and its allocations torn down; when the session ends, the whole room can be revoked.
- The gateway's secret never leaves Dani's machine and her app's own page.

## What a gateway refuses

A TURN server on someone's home machine must not become a proxy into their home. Freehop's gateway:

- refuses loopback, link-local, multicast and private peer addresses by default;
- keeps services on the host itself unreachable (the machine's own addresses only work relay to relay);
- bounds unauthenticated UDP errors and Binding answers per source and globally to limit reflection;
- accepts at most 16 TCP connections per source address, with a 10-second deadline to authenticate;
- caps usage at 6 allocations per username, 4 Mbit/s per allocation and 40 Mbit/s in total.

Media stays DTLS-SRTP encrypted end to end, so the gateway relays ciphertext it cannot read. The TURN realm is `peerlane`, Freehop's codename, which is why that name appears on the wire.

## A front door for the whole session

A gateway can also help two other people. Ben and Kai are both behind random-port NATs, so they cannot reach each other, but both can reach Dani's gateway. When their own pair runs out of options, the [session phase](./paths.mdx) adds Dani's gateway to their ICE servers. Each of them allocates a relay on it, and the gateway delivers traffic between its own allocations internally, without depending on the router's hairpinning. Their path is reported as `relay` via Dani (lab scenario `hard-pair-gateway`).

That traffic uses Dani's upload. It stays inside the session, and the quotas above bound it.

## Using it in your app

The [Electron helper](../sdk/desktop.mdx) runs the gateway in the main process and exposes it to your page. `connect()` finds it automatically, allows the room on it and offers it to the room's peers with per-peer credentials. In other Node.js programs, `startGateway()` from `freehop/relay` gives you the same gateway directly.

Electron 28 and newer can alternatively pin WebRTC's UDP port range (`webContents.setWebRTCUDPPortRange`) and map that range directly. Freehop's gateway approach needs no cooperation from Chromium.
